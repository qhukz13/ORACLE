# OQ-18 resolved: the mechanism reaches the ceiling, and the gate is still missed

**Date:** 2026-09-10 · **Run:** 2026-09-09 20:51 → 2026-09-10 02:46, 18,682 chunks at 0.88 chunks/s ·
**Data:** `logs/measurements/oq18-translated.{json,txt}`

OQ-18 asked: *can a Russian question reach an English codebase well enough to meet the 80% gate?*

**Answers, in order of how much they change what we do:**

1. **Query translation works, and the cheap mechanism is as good as the expensive one.**
2. **The 80% gate is still missed** — best arm 68%, so the Phase 5 recall criterion stays unmet.
3. **The best arm is not the lever this question was opened to test**, and the two levers have never
   been composed.
4. **One fixture is structurally unanswerable**, for a reason that generalises well past it.

## The numbers

Gate: **recall@5 ≥ 80%**. 38 fixtures, 25 of them Russian. Corpus 1,738 docs / 27,967 chunks
(18,682 embeddable).

|---|---|---|---|---|

## 1 · The mechanism reaches the ceiling

`dense_mt` and `dense_xl` are **identical where it counts**: 66% overall and 64% RU-only, both.

That comparison is the whole reason both arms exist. `_xl` embeds the *human* translations in the
fixture file — a number no running system can produce. `_mt` embeds what the resident `qwen3.5:0.8b`
actually emitted, recorded by `scripts/translate_fixtures.py`. P9-T2 had only the ceiling, and
shipping on it would have been shipping on a number the product cannot reach.

It turns out there is nothing to lose: **an 0.8b model's translation is as good as a human's, for
retrieval.** The two differ at r@1 (42% vs 37%) and r@10 (76% vs 79%) in opposite directions, which
at n=38 is noise, not a trend.

So there is **no headroom left in better translation** — a larger translator would buy nothing. That
is a useful negative result: it closes a direction rather than opening one.

**`Settings.translate_queries` stays `True`**, now confirmed by measurement rather than assumed:
translation lifts dense from 61% → 66% overall and, where it was aimed, **56% → 64% on Russian**.

## 2 · The gate is still missed

Best arm is **68%** against **80%**. The 2026-08-24 baseline was 61%, so the gap narrowed from 19
points to 12 — real progress, and not enough.

**Phase 5's recall criterion remains unmet, and OQ-18's own instruction stands: say so rather than
moving the gate to where the numbers already are.**

## 3 · The best arm is not the translation lever, and nobody has composed the two

`rrf_w2` — weighted RRF, **no translation at all** — scores **68%**, beating both translation arms
at 66%.

And there is **no `rrf_w2_mt` arm.** The two things that each help have never been measured together.
That is the obvious next experiment and it is nearly free: the forward pass is cached in
`D:/ORACLE/scratch/oq18-vectors-bge-m3.npz`, so a re-score costs seconds rather than six hours.

**Naive fusion actively hurts translation.** `rrf_mt` (58%) is worse than `dense_mt` (66%) *and*
worse than plain `dense` (61%). The reason is in the breakdown:

| arm | crosslang r@5 | semantic r@5 | lexical r@5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| dense | 0.56 | 0.60 | 1.00 |
| hybrid | 0.48 | 0.80 | 1.00 |
| lexical | **0.00** | 0.70 | 1.00 |

**BM25 contributes exactly zero on cross-language queries** — obviously, a Russian question shares no
terms with English source. So fusing it into a good dense ranking can only dilute it. Hybrid buys a
lot on *semantic* queries (0.60 → 0.80) and pays for it on *crosslang* (0.56 → 0.48).

That is an argument for **language-aware fusion**, and the `gated` arm was supposed to be exactly
that — it scores 61%, identical to plain `rrf`. **The gate is not doing its job**, and that is worth
looking at before adding another fusion variant.

## 4 · `en-relay-dockerfile` is unanswerable by construction

It misses in **every single arm** — dense, rrf, rrf_w2, gated, dense_xl, rrf_xl, dense_mt, rrf_mt.
That is not a ranking failure, and unlike `en-relay-secret-rotation` (fixed yesterday) the file is
present: `C:/Projects/Asterim/Dockerfile.relay` exists, is indexed, and the run printed *"all 38
fixture sources present"*.

The query is *"where do we configure the relay Dockerfile"*. Both halves of hybrid retrieval are
structurally unable to answer it:

* **Dense cannot.** `classify()` maps anything starting with `Dockerfile` to `ContentKind.CONFIG`,
  and `CONFIG.embeddable` is `False` — RAG.md §2 never embeds config, because an embedding of a
  tsconfig matches everything. **The document has no vector and never will.**
* **Lexical cannot reach it by name.** `chunks_fts` declares `rel_path UNINDEXED`, and
  `identifiers()` explodes identifiers out of the chunk **text**, never the path. **A document's
  filename is not searchable at all.**

So the only route left is the file's prose, and "relay" appears across the whole Asterim relay
subsystem — far too unselective to rank a Dockerfile first.

**Decision: keep the fixture, and do not reclassify it.** Marking it `kind: lexical` would move the
failure into the lexical bucket (currently a clean 1.00) without making it findable, which is
cosmetics. It is a true negative and it should keep failing until the underlying gap is closed.

**The generalisation is the finding**: ORACLE cannot answer *"where is X configured"* for any config
file, and cannot find **any** document by its filename. That is bigger than one fixture and gets its
own question (OQ-26).

## What this run cost, and one caveat on its numbers

5.9 hours at 0.88 chunks/s, against **2.52 chunks/s** measured on 2026-08-29. The machine was not
idle: the eval got 13.65 of 24 cores, with ~6.7 taken by ordinary desktop apps, while ONNX Runtime
was configured `intra_op_num_threads=24`. The 2026-08-29 figure was taken at 04:00 on a machine
woken by `WakeToRun` with nobody using it.

**So `chunks_per_s` from this run is not comparable to the earlier one and should not be quoted as a
regression.** The recall figures are unaffected — they are a function of what was embedded, not how
fast.

Also not comparable across runs: the corpus grew from 18,153 to 27,967 chunks since the 2026-08-26
baseline, partly because ORACLE indexes itself and this project commits a lot. Arm-vs-arm comparison
within this run is sound; absolute recall against the older runs is not.
